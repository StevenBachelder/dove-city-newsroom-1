# City Newsroom — Alignment with the Research Literature

*How the game's design embodies, operationalizes, and extends the cited sources. Written for the project team; suitable as an appendix to research documentation.*

City Newsroom places the player at the tip desk of a combined TV-and-print newsroom the day after it was physically attacked. Across three levels of increasing difficulty, the player verifies fifteen incoming tips using text search and reverse image search, pins evidence, and stamps PUBLISH or FLAG AS FALSE under a one-mistake-per-level tolerance. Feedback arrives at four timescales: immediately (verdict + technique card), per level (level report with strengths, focus areas, and badges), at the end (debrief, badge case, applicant report), and ambiently (meters, reader letters, ripple consequences).

---

## 1. Wineburg & McGrew (2019) — Lateral reading

**The source:** professional fact-checkers outperform historians and students not by reading *harder* but by reading *elsewhere* — leaving the page to see what the wider web says about a source, rather than evaluating the page on its own terms.

**In the game:** this is the core mechanic, not a message. The tip can never be verified from inside itself; the player *must* leave it — the Text Search and Reverse Image Search tools are the only path to a defensible verdict. The Text Search tool guide teaches it explicitly ("Read laterally. Open what other sources say about the claim instead of staying inside the story that made it"), and Sarah's taped checklist operationalizes Wineburg & McGrew's click-restraint and source-questioning as five checks present at the exact moment of decision. The game also rewards the *negative* lateral finding — "Silence is a clue" — which the authors identify as expert behavior. Evidence pins with per-source point values make the quality of lateral reading visible and scoreable.

## 2. Roozenbeek & van der Linden (2019) — Inoculation via serious games

**The source:** the *Bad News* game confers psychological resistance by exposing players to weakened doses of manipulation techniques (technique-based prebunking), improving detection across novel items.

**In the game:** the badge taxonomy *is* a technique-level inoculation curriculum — manipulated media, out-of-context, conspiracy framing, unverified claims, polarization, fabricated event/hoax, emotion ≠ false, plus ad recognition. Where *Bad News* teaches from the perpetrator's chair, City Newsroom teaches from the defender's chair; the two are pedagogically complementary (production-side vs. gatekeeping-side inoculation). Techniques recur across levels in escalating forms (e.g., a crudely edited photo at Level 1 returns as a fully AI-generated image at Level 3), which matches the inoculation logic of repeated, strengthening exposure. The Field Guide's encounter-based reveal converts each exposure into a durable reference entry.

## 3. Axelsson, Nygren, Roozenbeek & van der Linden (2024) — Serious gameplay in the civics classroom

**The source:** classroom deployment of *Bad News* with teenagers improves discernment of misinformation techniques; embedding gameplay in civics instruction works, and technique-level measurement is feasible with adolescents.

**In the game:** City Newsroom is built for exactly this deployment context — a 20–40 minute, browser-based, no-login experience in four languages, with the school-facing scaffolds the study implies: the level reports function as formative assessment ("You caught: … / Study before the next level: …"), and the downloadable Applicant Report is a per-student artifact listing technique-by-technique performance — usable directly as a classroom debrief document or a research data point. The one-mistake level gate adds a mastery-learning structure (retry with restored state) that the *Bad News* format does not have, and which the project can evaluate as a design contribution.

## 4. Falkheimer & Pamment (2026) — Psychological defence: theory and practice

**The source:** the Swedish Psychological Defence Agency's textbook systematizes information influence — actors, techniques, target audiences, and societal resilience as a whole-of-society capability.

**In the game:** the fiction models an influence *operation*, not scattered fakes: TAD (Tactical Advance Division) is an organized non-state actor; the storming case shows the operation's signature move — a cropped frame of a real event, amplified by coordinated accounts, arriving *fresher than the event itself*. The player is cast as a node of societal resilience (a gatekeeper whose individual discipline has collective consequences, made visible through ripple effects and reader letters). The game's vocabulary — techniques, target emotions, amplification — is compatible with the textbook's framework, so classroom use can bridge directly from gameplay to the MPF terminology. (Mapping here is at framework level; a terminology cross-walk against the final textbook chapters is a recommended next step.)

## 5. Hedling (2025) — Social identities and democratic vulnerabilities

**The source:** targeted disinformation exploits group identity — the vulnerability is not ignorance but belonging; effective influence content is *identity-flavored*.

**In the game:** the polarization case is a direct dramatization: a mundane scene rewritten with an enemy inside it ("us-versus-them stories," as the badge back says), amplified through an astroturf channel. The tips-desk guide teaches the identity mechanism at the personal level: "If a tip makes you feel strongly, that is the signal to slow down — your feelings are the target." The Level-3 fake-heavy structure tests whether players can hold discipline when content is engineered for their reflexes. A design note for future iterations: Hedling's argument suggests value in at least one case where the manipulated identity is one the *player* plausibly shares (youth culture, fandom), not only civic/ethnic framings.

## 6. Ranstorp & Ahlerup (2023) — The LVU campaign

**The source:** a documented Swedish case of a conspiracy-driven campaign linking domestic grievance networks with international amplification, run by non-state actors against a state institution.

**In the game:** the TAD arc is a fictionalized structural cousin: a non-state actor, a grievance narrative, coordinated inauthentic accounts (the CityTruth-style echo posts), and an attack on an institution of trust — here a newsroom rather than social services. The storming case's evidence set teaches the campaign anatomy the report documents: original footage vs. weaponized crop, organic vs. coordinated spread, and the tell that the amplification post-dates and reframes the event. The game deliberately keeps the actor organizational rather than ethnic or religious, which respects the classroom setting while preserving the structural lesson.

## 7. Säkerhetspolisen (2026) — Lägesbild 2025–2026

**The source:** the security service's situational assessment of threats to Sweden, including hybrid and influence activities and threats against societal institutions.

**In the game:** the day-before attack on the City News building grounds the fiction in the threat picture such assessments describe: media houses as targets, information attack paired with physical intimidation, and the normalization of pressure against journalists. The game's premise — that verification is practiced *inside* a threatened institution — gives adolescents an affective, non-abstract entry into why "psychological defence" exists. (As with source 4, alignment is thematic; the game does not depict any specific actor named in the assessment, by design.)

## 8. Siegel (2020) — Online hate speech

**The source:** defines and maps online hate speech, its measurement, and its relationship to platform dynamics.

**In the game:** the most honest statement is that City Newsroom *borders* this literature rather than implements it. The polarization and storming cases show hate-adjacent dynamics — dehumanizing framing, enemy construction, brigading — but the game deliberately contains no actual hate speech, keeping content classroom-safe. Siegel's chapter is best used by the project as a boundary reference: it explains what the game consciously stops short of, and why the polarization case teaches the *mechanism* (enemy written into a scene) without reproducing the *content*.

## Research affordances the game already provides

- **Technique-level discernment data:** every verdict is recorded per case with technique labels — directly comparable to the discernment measures in sources 1 and 3.
- **Process data, not just outcomes:** tool usage (text/image search), evidence pinned (with quality values), ad-pin avoidance, and time-order of actions capture *how* players verify, speaking to source 8's measurement questions and Wineburg & McGrew's process focus.
- **Mastery dynamics:** the retry system yields within-player repeated measures on identical items — a design opening for learning-curve analysis absent from single-pass games.
- **Artifacts for mixed methods:** the Applicant Report and level reports give per-participant documents for qualitative follow-up interviews.

## Suggested next steps

1. Pre/post discernment instrument mirroring Axelsson et al. (2024), using held-out tip items in the same visual format.
2. Terminology cross-walk of the badge taxonomy against Falkheimer & Pamment's final technique typology.
3. One identity-targeted case per Hedling, reviewed with the youth language panel.
4. Log-export hook (anonymous JSON of the existing state object) to make the affordances above collectable in classroom studies.
