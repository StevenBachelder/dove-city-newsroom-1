# City Newsroom × James Paul Gee — Learning-Principle Alignment

*Companion to RESEARCH_ALIGNMENT.md. Maps the game's mechanics to Gee's learning principles (What Video Games Have to Teach Us About Learning and Literacy, 2003; "Good Video Games and Good Learning," 2005). Written so it can be cited in design documentation: principle → where it lives in the game.*

**Identity.** Gee argues deep learning requires taking on a projective identity worth caring about. The player is not "doing a media-literacy exercise"; they are a trial applicant at a wounded newsroom, addressed by name on a press card, judged by a named editor. The boarded window and Sarah's letter give the identity stakes before the first mechanic appears.

**Psychosocial moratorium (risk-taking with lowered consequences).** The retry system is this principle made mechanical: real-world verification failures cost reputations; here a failed level costs one do-over and restores the world (meters, badges, queue) to its level-start state. Students can be brave precisely because the game is safe — and the *polite* door ending keeps even total failure re-invitational ("come back sharper").

**Well-ordered problems + cycles of expertise (challenge and consolidation).** The three-level structure with recurring techniques is a textbook Gee expertise cycle: a skill is learned in a simple form (Level 1's crude photo edit), consolidated, then deliberately destabilized by a harder variant (Level 3's fully AI-generated image; "the fakes stop looking fake"). The 5/5/5 deck with a uniform one-mistake bar makes each cycle's mastery test identical in shape and rising in content.

**Pleasantly frustrating / regime of competence.** The one-wrong-tolerance keeps players at the edge of their ability: perfection is not required (one slip is absorbed), but sloppiness is not survivable. The mistake chip (✗ 0/1) keeps the pressure legible rather than anxious — Gee's point that good frustration is *felt as fair*.

**Performance before competence.** Players stamp verdicts from tip one, long before they could pass a quiz on manipulation techniques. Competence is built *by* performing with scaffolds (the taped checklist, the tool guides) rather than gated behind instruction.

**"On demand" and "just in time" information.** Almost nothing is taught up front. The three best-practice guides open from the station emblems *when wanted*; the Field Guide reveals technique entries *when encountered*; Sarah's checklist sits on the board *at the moment of stamping*; the technique card appears *right after* the verdict it explains. The intro rewrite (moving the psychology lecture out of the memo and into the Field Guide) was explicitly a Gee-ward move.

**Situated meaning.** "Out of context," "unverified," "polarization" are never defined in the abstract first; each word gets its meaning from a lived case (the storming crop, the footballer's non-confirmed test, the enemy written into the forest fires) and only then becomes a badge-card definition. Gee: words mean through experiences, not definitions.

**Probe → hypothesize → reprobe → rethink.** The core loop is literally this cycle: read the tip (probe), form a hunch, search (reprobe), pin evidence, and the collapsible search sections + three-pin limit force *rethinking* which evidence actually carries the verdict.

**Smart tools and distributed knowledge.** The player's competence is deliberately distributed across tools (text search, reverse image search), artifacts (the evidence board, the pinned sources), and the environment (the checklist note). Being a good verifier *is* knowing how to lean on the toolkit — which is precisely the transferable real-world skill.

**Multimodality / semiotic domains.** Verdicts are taught through text, image, sound (the approving vs. rejecting stamps, the rising resolution grammar), color (green/red evidence frames), and motion (the score wiggle). The game treats verification as a semiotic domain with its own signs — sender lines, buzz meters, date stamps — and teaches students to *read* that domain, Gee's core literacy claim.

**Agency and consequentiality ("actions matter").** Meters move, ripples plant tomorrow's fakes from today's mistakes, readers write letters, and the front page prints what the player chose. Gee's "amplification of input": one stamp produces a world of visible response.

**Production, not just consumption (partial).** Players co-produce their record — the pinned evidence set, the Applicant Report, the badge collection are artifacts of their choices. The game stops short of full Gee-style production (players don't *create* misinformation, by ethical design — the perpetrator seat is Bad News' territory); this is a deliberate boundary worth stating in research framing.

**Affinity space (design opening).** Gee's affinity-group principle is the least implemented: play is solo. The teacher compendium's pair-play recommendation and classroom debrief partially supply it; a future "compare your press cards" or class-leaderboard-of-discipline feature would complete it and is noted in the backlog.

**Summary for citation.** City Newsroom operationalizes at least eleven of Gee's principles as mechanics rather than messages, with identity, moratorium-protected risk, expertise cycles, just-in-time information, and situated meaning as the load-bearing five. The two principled *departures* — no perpetrator-side production, limited affinity structures — are ethical and deployment choices, not omissions, and pair the game complementarily with production-side interventions (Roozenbeek & van der Linden) in a Gee-consistent larger curriculum.
